# Module 9: Demo Day

**⏱ Time: ~30 minutes | 🔴 Difficulty: Advanced | 💻 Coding: None**

You built something. Now show the world.

There's a weird thing that happens when you build in private: it doesn't feel real. You can have the coolest agent on the planet, but if nobody sees it, did you really make it? (Yes. But also — share it anyway.)

Showing your work isn't vanity. It's the final step of building. It forces you to explain what you made, why it matters, and what it actually does. And in the process, you'll understand your own creation better than you did before.

Let's make that demo.

---

## Why Demos Matter

You've spent hours building, debugging, polishing, and documenting your agent. You could just push it to GitHub and walk away. Some people do. But here's what they miss:

- **Feedback you didn't know you needed.** Someone will see your demo and say "have you tried using it for X?" — and X will be something brilliant you never considered.
- **Connections.** People who build things attract other people who build things. Your demo is a bat signal.
- **Proof of work.** In a world where everyone *talks* about AI, you actually *built* something. A demo is the receipt.
- **Practice.** Explaining technical work to humans is a superpower. Every demo makes you better at it.

## The Problem → Solution → Demo → Impact Framework

Every great demo follows this arc. Not because it's a formula — because it's how humans naturally understand things.

### 1. The Problem (30 seconds)

Start with the pain. What annoyed you? What took too long? What was broken?

> "Every morning I'd spend 15 minutes checking the weather, my calendar, and three news sites before I could start my day. It was boring and it was the same routine every single time."

Keep it relatable. If your audience has felt the same frustration, you've already got them.

### 2. The Solution (30 seconds)

What did you build? One sentence. No jargon.

> "So I built a morning briefing agent that checks all of that for me and gives me a 30-second summary the moment I wake up."

Don't explain how it works yet. Just say what it does. The "how" is for the demo.

### 3. The Demo (90 seconds)

This is the main event. Show the thing working. Live, or in a recording. Let people see the actual experience — the conversation, the output, the magic moment.

Show, don't tell:
- Show the agent starting up
- Show a real interaction (not a rehearsed one — keep it natural)
- Show the output that solves the problem from step 1
- If something surprising or delightful happens, let it breathe

### 4. The Impact (30 seconds)

Bring it home. What changed?

> "Now my mornings start in 30 seconds instead of 15 minutes. I've been using it every day for two weeks. It even reminded me about a dentist appointment I forgot I had."

Real impact is better than theoretical impact. "I saved 15 minutes a day" beats "this could theoretically save millions of hours."

**✅ Try it now!** Write your four-part framework on paper or in a note. One sentence for the Problem, one for the Solution, three bullets for what you'll show in the Demo, one sentence for Impact. Keep the whole thing under 10 sentences.

## The 3-Minute Demo Structure

Three minutes. That's it. Here's why:

- Under 3 minutes: people will actually watch the whole thing
- Over 3 minutes: people start skipping around or clicking away
- Way over 3 minutes: people don't click play at all

### Minute-by-Minute Breakdown

| Time | What You're Doing |
|------|-------------------|
| 0:00–0:30 | The Problem — why you built this |
| 0:30–1:00 | The Solution — what it is (start screen share here) |
| 1:00–2:30 | The Demo — show it working, live |
| 2:30–3:00 | The Impact — what changed, where to find it |

### What to Show

- The actual conversation with your agent
- The moment it does something impressive
- Any personality that comes through (jokes, tone, quirks)
- The output: a briefing, a search result, a file it created, whatever

### What to Skip

- Installation (nobody wants to watch `npm install`)
- Configuration (show the running product, not the setup)
- Long loading screens (edit these out)
- Explaining code (this is a demo, not a lecture)
- Apologizing ("sorry if this is boring" — it's not, and now you've made it weird)

**✅ Try it now!** Practice your demo out loud. Time yourself. Cut anything that isn't essential. Get it under 3 minutes. Yes, it's possible — you just have to be ruthless about what stays and what goes.

## Recording Tips

You don't need fancy equipment. Here's the minimum viable recording setup:

### Screen Capture

- **Mac:** QuickTime Player → File → New Screen Recording (it's free and built in)
- **Windows:** Win+G opens the Game Bar recorder (also free and built in)
- **Any OS:** [OBS Studio](https://obsproject.com) is free and powerful

### Audio

- Use your laptop mic. Seriously. It's fine for a 3-minute demo.
- Find a quiet room. That matters more than the mic quality.
- Speak at a normal pace. When you're nervous, you speed up. Slow down.

### The Recording Checklist

Before you hit record:

- [ ] Close unnecessary tabs and apps (notifications WILL pop up at the worst moment)
- [ ] Increase your terminal font size so text is readable in the video
- [ ] Have your demo conversation planned but not scripted word-for-word
- [ ] Do one practice run all the way through
- [ ] Check that your audio is actually recording (test a 5-second clip first)

### When Things Go Wrong During Recording

They will. And that's honestly fine.

If your agent does something unexpected during recording, don't panic. Some of the best demos are the ones where something goes slightly off-script and the builder handles it naturally. It shows the agent is real, not a choreographed illusion.

If it truly crashes, just stop recording, fix it, and start over. Nobody has to know about take 7.

**✅ Try it now!** Do a test recording. Just 30 seconds — introduce yourself and your agent. Play it back. Check that the audio works, the screen is readable, and you don't hate the sound of your own voice (everyone hates it at first — push through).

## Where to Share

You've got a recording (or you're doing it live). Here's where to put it:

### GitHub
Add your demo video or a link to it in your repository's README. A repo with a demo video gets dramatically more attention than one without.

### ClawHub
If you published your skill to ClawHub, link the demo there too. People browsing skills want to see them in action.

### YouTube
Upload as an unlisted or public video. You don't need a channel brand or fancy thumbnails. Just a clear title: "I Built an AI Agent That [Does the Cool Thing] — 3-Minute Demo"

### X (Twitter)
Post a 60-second clip (or a screen recording GIF). Tag `@OpenClaw`. Thread format works well:

> 🧵 I just built an AI agent that does [thing].
>
> Here's a 60-second demo: [video]
>
> Built with @OpenClaw in [X] hours. Here's the source: [link]

### Discord
Share in the OpenClaw community. The #showcase channel exists for exactly this.

### Anywhere Else
Your blog, Reddit, Hacker News, a class presentation, show your friends over lunch. The medium doesn't matter. Putting it out there does.

**✅ Try it now!** Pick at least one platform. Record your demo. Post it. Right now. Not "later" — now. Later has a success rate of approximately 0%.

## Engaging with Feedback

You posted your demo. People are responding. Here's how to handle it:

**Positive feedback:** Say thank you. Don't deflect with "oh it's nothing." You built something real. Own it.

**Feature suggestions:** Write them down. You don't have to implement everything, but good ideas come from users more often than from builders.

**Criticism:** Separate "this doesn't work" (useful) from "this is dumb" (noise). The first one is a gift. The second one is someone else's problem.

**Silence:** Sometimes nobody responds. That's fine. The demo still exists. People will find it. Not everything goes viral, and that's not the point.
